Reading the Coverage Map

A/B testers could not say what the atlas section was telling them on a first read. Here is why, and a sequence that answers the question they actually brought.

Every number below is from one real scan. 1,498 records 68% English, 9% Turkish, 4% Chinese 1,464 placed of 1,498 sampled atlas v1-lite, 212 subregions in 48 subject areas

It teaches a new idea before it says anything familiar

The central number is density against a reference corpus. Understanding it requires knowing that a reference corpus exists, what is in it, and why a ratio against it is the interesting quantity — none of which the page has established.

first thing on screen: 212 ratios

Two different percentages sit in adjacent columns

Share is a share of your data. Density is a ratio against the map. They look alike and mean opposite things, so a reader who conflates them concludes exactly the wrong thing about what to add.

11.9% share · 4.8× density · same row, same alignment

The cells are anonymous

dropoutt's own rule is that a place is named by the reader's record before it is named by a caption. The grid — the lead visual — is 212 unlabelled squares. The rule is followed everywhere except the first thing anyone looks at.

0 of 212 cells carry a record

Nothing says what to do

A grid of ratios is a measurement. The question a reader arrived with is what should I add, and answering it requires sorting 48 rows by a column that is not sorted.

rows ordered by the map, not by what to fix
02

The proposed sequence

Five moves, in order. Each one is answerable without the one after it, so a reader can stop at any point and still be holding something true. The grid does not disappear — it stops being the opening.

Move 01

One sentence, in their units

No ratios, no reference corpus, no vocabulary to learn. Counts and a shape.

proposed

Your data reaches 177 of the map's 212 neighbourhoods — but thinly. At the reference corpus's own density it would cover 107.

Six subject areas hold 57% of it. The other 42 share the rest.

Move 02

One coverage bar

212 segments, one per neighbourhood, each as tall as your density there. "How much of the map do I touch, and how thinly" in a single glance, with no legend to read first.

proposed
densest neighbourhood, 9.2× the map 35 never reached
Move 03

The extremes, labelled with their own records

Diverging bars are read correctly by people who have never seen one. Each row carries one of the reader's own records from that place, and an instruction rather than a measurement.

03

Two changes worth making either way

  • Separate the two percentages visually. Share-of-yours and density-vs-map must not be adjacent right-aligned columns of the same weight. Density belongs in a different register — a bar, a chip, a colour — so the two cannot be read as the same kind of number even by someone skimming.
  • Say what the reference corpus is, in one line, above the first comparison. Its version, its size and roughly what is in it currently live in a footer, below every number that depends on knowing them.
04

How to know whether it worked

The current test asked people whether they liked it. Preference is not the failure being diagnosed — comprehension is, and it is measurable. Show the section, then ask one question:

Name one subject area you should add more of, and one you have too much of.

Score the share who answer correctly, and the seconds they take. That is the thing this section exists to make possible, and it is the measurement that would have caught the current design before it shipped.
